# Article Writing Guide

This is a practical guide in writing the article `./article.md`.

## Guide Questions

1. **What is your opening?**
   - Identify a larger problem which you are contributing
   - Give the reader a sense of direction
   - Make it clear WHY this is important

2. **What is the specific question or hypothesis?**

3. **What are the key results?**
   - Identify a short list: 2 to 3 points

4. **What is the conclusion or takeaway?**
   - What did you learn?
   - Use the results from 3 to answer the question from 2
   - Address the larger problem identified in 1

## Goal

800 - 850 words (max)
- Tell the story of the work, engage and educate readers
- Do not write a technical paper; tone can range from casual to somewhat technical

## Positioning an Article and a Research Paper

This note captures the working distinction between the planned [personal case study](./article.md) and a journal research paper. It also records how the ideas from *Writing Science* relate to the structure and length of this work.

### Structure and story are compatible

IMRAD—Introduction, Methods, Results and Discussion—provides boundaries for where information belongs. A story connects those sections: it helps the reader follow why the question matters, how it was investigated, what was found and what the findings mean.

These are not competing approaches. IMRAD can be the structure while the story provides the flow within and between sections. A personal article may use adapted headings such as Approach and Observations instead of Methods and Results.

### The difference is positioning

Both formats benefit from clear questions, evidence, relevant sources and honest limitations. The difference is primarily the intended contribution and the reader's expectations.

| | Personal case-study article | Journal research paper |
|---|---|---|
| **Purpose** | Show what I investigated and learned from my case. | Explain how an investigation contributes to a question others are studying. |
| **Literature** | Cite sources that support background claims or help interpret observations. | Position the question and findings more explicitly against existing research: what is known, what remains uncertain and what the work adds. |
| **Methods** | Describe what I did clearly enough to understand the observations and their limitations. | Generally give enough detail for readers and reviewers to assess—and, where practical, reproduce—the approach. |
| **Findings** | Present the relevant observations and what I learned, without claiming more than the case supports. | Report and analyse findings in a form appropriate to the research question and journal, then interpret them in context. |

These are working distinctions, not hard categories: expectations vary by outlet and article type. A personal article can be detailed and well referenced, while some journal formats are brief.

For this case study, the aim is to explain the question, approach, observations, learning and limitations. A single person's records do not, on their own, establish device accuracy or show that the same experience applies to everyone.

### Detail and length

Include details because they help the reader understand, assess or interpret the work—not to make it longer. For example, device make and model, what a device measures or estimates, recording frequency and changes in settings may matter. A list of specifications that does not affect the analysis may not.

A journal paper is often longer because it has more context, methods or reporting to cover, or because its outlet requires them. It is not automatically more credible because it has more words. If a concise article adequately explains and supports its claims, it does not need to be lengthened for its own sake.

The 800–850-word goal and section allocations in the [structure guide](./article-structure-guide.md) are **optional drafting constraints**, not requirements or measures of quality. Draft the necessary account first; assess its length afterwards. If a hard limit conflicts with necessary detail, options include narrowing the question or placing supporting methods and data in an appendix.

### Working with the existing data

The data has already been collected, but the analysis has not yet started. The story can provide a provisional frame for the question; it should not determine the findings in advance.

Before analysis, identify:

1. The question the existing data might help answer.
2. The records available, their time period and material gaps.
3. The comparisons or trends that could address the question, and what they cannot establish.

Let the analysis shape the eventual account. A clear pattern, an uncertain result or an inability to answer the original question can all be reported honestly.

### Publication and credibility

A possible route is to publish the readable article on Substack and deposit a dated version on Zenodo for citation. A DOI identifies a work; it does not certify its quality or imply peer review. Credibility comes from transparent methods, appropriate sources, supported conclusions and clearly stated limitations.
