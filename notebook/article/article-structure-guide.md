# Article Structure Guide

This is a drafting scaffold for [the article](./article.md). Use the headings as **hard boundaries** for a first draft: each section answers a different reader question. The word allocations are starting constraints, not fixed rules; adjust them if the story needs more space in one section than another.

The overall sequence is:

**Why this question? → What must I know? → What did you do? → What happened? → What does it mean? → What is the answer?**

## Introduction — about 120 words

**Reader's question:** Why should I care?

**Start:** State the everyday problem: energy balance is useful in theory, but difficult to measure outside controlled conditions.

**Include:** Why this matters and the question the case study asks.

**Stop:** Once you have stated what the article will examine. Do not explain the full tracking process yet.

**Transition:** “To explore this question, I tracked […] using […].”

## Background — about 125 words

**Reader's question:** What do I need to understand?

**Start:** Explain what the reader needs to know about energy balance.

**Include:** Intake, estimated expenditure, and why weight is better interpreted as a trend than as a single reading.

**Stop:** Once the reader can understand the quantities you tracked. Do not explain every aspect of nutrition or exercise.

**Transition:** “With these concepts in mind, I set up […] to track my data.”

## Approach — about 155 words

**Reader's question:** What did you do?

**Start:** Say when and for how long you tracked.

**Include:** The tools, what you recorded, and how you compared the data.

**Stop:** Once the reader understands how you produced the observations. Do not interpret the results yet.

**Transition:** “The resulting records showed […].”

## Observations — about 180 words

**Reader's question:** What did you see?

**Start:** Present the most important pattern you observed.

**Include:** Two or three relevant observations, supported by figures or concrete examples from your records.

**Stop:** Once you have shown what happened. Save broader explanations and claims for the discussion.

**Transition:** “These observations suggest […], although […] limits what can be concluded.”

## Discussion — about 175 words

**Reader's question:** What does it mean?

**Start:** Interpret your most important observation.

**Include:** What the data helped you understand or decide; limitations of the estimates; and plausible alternative explanations.

**Stop:** Once you have explained what the observations do and do not support. Do not introduce a new major result here.

## Conclusion — about 70 words

**Reader's question:** What is the answer?

**Start:** Return to the question from the introduction.

**Include:** Your qualified answer and one practical takeaway.

**Stop:** Once you have answered the question. Do not introduce new evidence.

## Applying the scaffold

The allocations total approximately **825 words**, within the 800–850-word goal in the [article writing guide](./article-writing-guide.md).

Add **Approach** between Background and Observations, and **Conclusion** after Discussion. Temporarily remove the background subheadings on NEAT, weight mechanics, macronutrients and exercise. Bring any of them back within the relevant section if the observations need them.

To distinguish observations from discussion, ask: **“Could I point directly to this in my records?”** If so, it is probably an observation. If it explains, qualifies, or draws a lesson from the records, it is probably discussion.

The transition sentences are cues, not required wording. Replace the placeholders with details supported by the case study.
